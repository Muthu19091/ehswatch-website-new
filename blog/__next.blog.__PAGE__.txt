5:T694,<p>Walk into ten EHS team rooms and nine of them have a dashboard counting incidents, lost-time days and recordables. Those numbers matter — but they are all <strong>lagging indicators</strong>. By the time the chart moves, someone has already been hurt.</p><p>The teams genuinely pulling incident rates down focus their leadership attention on <strong>leading indicators</strong>: near-miss reports per worker, safety observation rate, training currency, action close-out speed, audit finding ageing. These metrics shift weeks or months before the lagging ones do — they tell you what is <em>going</em> to happen, not what already did.</p><h3>Three leading indicators every dashboard should have</h3><p><strong>1. Observations per worker per month.</strong> A worker logging one positive or at-risk observation a month is engaged with safety. A workforce averaging below 0.3 is not. This metric correlates more strongly with incident rate than any audit score we have measured.</p><p><strong>2. Action close-out cycle time.</strong> Median days from finding raised to action verified-closed. When this drifts above 30 days, your actions are not actions — they are wishes.</p><p><strong>3. Training currency.</strong> Percentage of workers whose certifications are current for the roles they are performing today. Anything below 95% is an audit finding waiting to happen.</p><h3>How to make the switch</h3><p>Add the leading indicators to your existing dashboard <em>alongside</em> the lagging ones — do not replace, augment. Set targets for the leading metrics first; the lagging ones will follow. And review them at the same cadence and forum you currently review TRIFR.</p>6:T702,<p>Across the 450+ sites running EHSWatch, one number predicts injury rate better than any other we have measured: <strong>median days from action created to action verified-closed</strong>.</p><p>Sites in the top quartile (under 14 days) report 47% fewer recordable injuries than sites in the bottom quartile (over 60 days). The correlation holds across industry, geography and company size.</p><h3>Why close-out time matters more than action count</h3><p>An open action is a known hazard with a known owner who has not yet fixed it. The longer it stays open, the more shifts and workers it exposes. A site generating 200 actions a year but closing them in 10 days is genuinely safer than a site generating 500 actions a year and closing them in 90.</p><p>This is also the metric that fails audits most often. Auditors do not ask "did you find the hazard" — they ask "where is the evidence you fixed it". An overdue action register is the single most damning artefact in any audit.</p><h3>Four ways to compress close-out cycle time</h3><p><strong>1. One accountable owner per action — always.</strong> "The team" is not an owner. "The night shift" is not an owner. Tom Henderson is an owner.</p><p><strong>2. Require evidence before closing.</strong> A photo, a sign-off, an attestation — something concrete. Self-attested closures with no evidence are the leakiest part of any action register.</p><p><strong>3. Visible overdue list at the right altitude.</strong> Site leads should see their team's overdue actions every morning. Regional leads should see the per-site overdue counts every week.</p><p><strong>4. SLAs by severity, not blanket.</strong> A trip hazard does not need closing in the same window as a confined-space violation. Bucket your actions and run different SLAs.</p>7:T6bf,<h2>Why near-misses go unreported</h2><p>Most EHS teams understand why near-miss reporting matters — it's the leading indicator that prevents tomorrow's incident. The challenge isn't convincing people of the value; it's consistently capturing those events in a way that leads to meaningful follow-through.</p><p>In our work with safety teams across construction, manufacturing and logistics, three barriers come up again and again:</p><ol><li><strong>Reporting feels punitive.</strong> Workers fear repercussions even when leadership says otherwise. The reporting form itself often reinforces this — long, lawyer-vetted, demands details no one has at 4pm on a Friday.</li><li><strong>No one sees what happened next.</strong> A near-miss goes into a black box. Workers stop reporting because they never see the outcome.</li><li><strong>The same patterns recur.</strong> Patterns across sites are invisible until someone manually correlates them, by which point the next incident has already occurred.</li></ol><h2>What works instead</h2><p>The three changes that move the needle:</p><ul><li><strong>30-second mobile capture.</strong> A reporter who has to open a laptop and log into a portal is a reporter who doesn't report. The first form field should accept a voice note or a photo.</li><li><strong>Public follow-through.</strong> Every reporter sees what happened to their report — and so does their team.</li><li><strong>Cross-site pattern detection.</strong> One observation isn't a pattern. Twelve observations across three sites that share a root cause is a pattern — and your platform should surface it for you.</li></ul><p>These three changes don't need a new safety policy.
